Israel’s 2035 renewable electricity debate: a proposal is a stage, not a result

The Electricity Authority’s January recommendation and the ministry’s May strategy draft are separate steps in planning the next decade. Here is how to read their dates, percentages and status.

Select an underlined term for a short explanation.

Planning electricity supply involves decisions whose consequences extend well beyond the day of an announcement. In January 2026, Israel’s Electricity Authority presented a recommendation for 35% renewable electricity generation in 2035. Its official announcement described publication for public responses; the accompanying Public consultationA process for seeking comments on a proposed policy before completing the decision process. page was published on 19 January. The central fact is therefore a documented recommendation in a policy process, not evidence that the country has already reached that share.

The year belongs beside the percentage. Moving from a 30% benchmark to 35% represents five Percentage pointsThe arithmetic difference between two percentages;35% minus30% is five percentage points., but those figures refer to different target years when comparing 2030 and 2035. That simple arithmetic does not establish the yearly construction pace, the amount of equipment needed or the electricity produced. To answer those questions, a reader would also need the demand assumptions, starting position and delivery timetable behind the proposal.

In May 2026, the Energy Ministry published a separate draft national renewable-energy strategy for 2035. Its introduction describes examining Israel’s position, international trends, barriers, development potential and alternative future mixes. The purpose was to turn those considerations into proposed actions. This is a Strategy draftA proposed framework for longer-term objectives and actions that remains open to revision. with a broader implementation focus; it should not be collapsed into the earlier Authority recommendation as though the two publications were one final decision.

The consultation dates help reconstruct the process. The Authority’s inspected page showed a 5 March 2026 submission deadline and was marked closed. The ministry’s strategy page gave 2 July 2026 as its response deadline after an update in June. A closed comment period tells us that the stated window has ended. It does not tell us which comments were accepted, whether the draft changed or whether a final target was subsequently adopted.

The next useful document would connect those stages: a final decision, its reasons and the implementation measures that follow. Readers can look for the responsible bodies, deadlines and a way to measure delivery, then compare those commitments with later evidence. The inspected pages support an account of the 2026 consultation process. They do not, by themselves, establish that a new 2035 target has replaced the adopted 2030 target.

One roof, many users

A warehouse roof powering three shopping centres. An orchard powering a fruit-sorting hall. Three countries show how to use solar where it’s needed, not only where it fits.

Select an underlined term for a short explanation.

Israel’s grid is turning away more than half of the new solar it’s asked to connect (see “Targets against delivery”). One response is to use power close to where it’s made, or share it between known users. Three projects from autumn 2026 show different ways to do that.

Cape Town: one roof, three buildings. Redefine Properties has commissioned a R106 million rooftop system on the Massmart distribution centre at Brackengate: 11,691 panels across about 57,000 m², rated at 7.014 MWp. The company expects about 9,889 MWh a year. The power is not used on site. It is Wheelingsending electricity from a generator to a named customer across a public grid, paying a network charge. through the City of Cape Town’s municipal grid to three other Redefine properties: Kenilworth Centre, Blue Route Mall and The Towers office precinct. The project is part of the city’s wheeling pilot and rests on a 20-year commitment. A warehouse with a huge roof and modest demand becomes a power plant for buildings with the opposite profile.

Kressbronn, Germany: orchard to sorting hall. On Lake Constance, Sonntag Energy inaugurated a 2.4 MW AgriPV system over apple trees in late October 2026, about ten times the size of the farmer’s 2022 test plot. The modules replace hail nets. A 500-metre Direct linea private cable from a generator to a nearby consumer, bypassing the public grid. supplies the BayWa Obst fruit-sorting hall next door. A 3.4 MWh battery charges when power is cheap and sells when it’s dear. According to the farmer’s account of the test plot, monitored by Fraunhofer ISE, it needed 70% less pesticide and half as much water. That is one site’s report, not a general result.

Australia: paying for the middle. From 1 October 2026, Australia’s Small-scale Renewable Energy Scheme covers systems up to 1 MW, up from 100 kW. The government estimates this cuts upfront costs for mid-size systems by about 20%. That size band covers factories, farms, schools and community buildings, the same “missing middle” that Israel’s low-voltage proposal would push into the market above 300–630 kW (see “The grid decides”). Australia is also seeking rule changes to ease grid connection for these larger systems.

Israel has pieces of each model: industrial roofs, packhouses beside orchards, the proposed limited-export storage route (see “Storage moves into planning”). It lacks a clear way to share one site’s power with a neighbour or with the owner’s other buildings. That is a regulatory gap SPFI will keep in view.

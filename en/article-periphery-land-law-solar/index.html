The periphery land law: who gets the profit?

A new law lets Israel’s poorer peripheral towns ask for state land for solar without a land tender. Environmental groups warned about open space; the final text tries to balance both.

Select an underlined term for a short explanation.

Israel’s solar boom has mostly enriched developers, landowners and the agricultural settlements that host fields. Peripheral towns, many of them low-income, often host the transmission lines but share little of the income. A July 2026 amendment to the Israel Land Authority Law tries to change that.

What the law allows. A local authority ranked 1 to 5 on the national Peripherality indexa Central Bureau of Statistics ranking of local authorities by distance from population centres., and which received an Interior Ministry Equalisation grantan Interior Ministry payment to local authorities with weak income bases. in each of the previous three years, may ask the Israel Land Authority for land within its boundaries for a renewable-electricity project. The land is allocated without a land tender. The council must still pick an experienced developer through a public tender, and the payment it receives cannot depend on the project’s profits.

When a town has no land. An eligible authority with no suitable site can apply jointly with a neighbouring, non-eligible authority. The land is then allocated in the neighbour’s area, but 80% of the project’s profit must go to the eligible town. Land counts as available only if it is a contiguous plot of at least 75 dunams.

The safeguards. Allocation requires a project that fits the statutory plan and holds grid-connection approval under the Electricity Sector Law. The town’s engineer must also submit an opinion on rooftop solar potential across its public buildings and a plan to use it. Project details must go on the council website within 14 days. When the project ends, the land returns to the state clear.

The argument behind the text. The Society for the Protection of Nature in Israel opposed the bill as an “easy solution” that could consume open land, habitats and ecological corridors, and pull solar away from roofs, parking lots and sports grounds. It reports that the final version cut the amount of land designated, added the planning and grid conditions, and required site restoration.

The law links solar to municipal income and regional inequality more directly than any previous Israeli measure. Whether it works depends on details not yet visible: how much land the ILA Council allows per town, which towns apply first, and whether projects favour disturbed land over open countryside. SPFI will track the first applications.

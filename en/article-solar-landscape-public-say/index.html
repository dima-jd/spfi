Who gets a say in the solar landscape

In New Zealand, a solar farm’s fate turned on fire risk, glare and a conflict of interest. In the Netherlands, researchers asked what makes a solar field feel like a landscape. Both lessons point to process.

Israel is about to plan AgriPV at landscape scale: 1,700 dunams in the Valley of Springs, border-region schemes in the Galilee and Negev (see “Energized is not operating”). Two cases abroad show what happens when the public is invited in, or left out.

Waipara, New Zealand: process as the story. Far North Solar Farm Ltd proposed a 181-hectare farm near Waipara in North Canterbury: about 250,000 panels and up to 144 MW. The regional council, Environment Canterbury, granted its consents without Public notificationpublishing a planning application so anyone can comment or object., despite a 700-signature petition asking for it. The district council did notify its part and received 158 submissions, 125 of them opposed. Residents raised landscape and visual effects, property values, glare for drivers, wind resistance and the area’s heritage. A council staff report found the landscape effects “more than minor”.

Then the process itself became the issue. Opponents showed that the fire-risk report had been written by a firm sharing directors with the applicant. The commissioners ordered an independent fire assessment from Stantec, delaying the decision. The applicant also withdrew 6.8 hectares after native matagouri shrubs were cleared on the site. The radar reports that commissioners granted consent in part on 13 October 2026.

Gelderland, Netherlands: designing for acceptance. Researchers Kitti Biró-Varga and Sven Stremke at Wageningen University studied Vertical AgriPVupright bifacial panels in rows, with farming in the strips between., in which bifacial panels stand upright between strips of farmland or pasture, in Dutch peat landscapes. Their January 2026 paper in Renewable Energy finds that perceived landscape quality improves when projects are accessible, include park-like functions and provide for biodiversity and wildlife, and when their visual appearance and screening are designed deliberately. The authors turn this into design guidelines and spatial-policy recommendations.

The two cases fit together. Waipara shows that independent evidence and genuine notification decide whether a project is trusted. Gelderland shows that access, habitat and form can be designed in from the start, rather than argued over at the hearing. Israel’s draft AgriPV rules measure crops; they say little about paths, views, fences or who gets to object. The new cooperative-approval rule (see “The kibbutz vote”) is a start for members. Neighbours and the wider public still have no equivalent.

Space solar power heads for a conversation with the energy industry

The Space Frontier Foundation plans a Washington symposium on 30 September 2026. Demonstrations and cost studies show why the conversation still centres on development.

Select an underlined term for a short explanation.

What would electricity buyers need to know before considering solar power collected in orbit? The Space Frontier Foundation plans to put that question before energy professionals at a half-day symposium in Washington, DC, on 30 September 2026. Its organiser page places the event at the Omni Shoreham, within Experience POWER. This SPFI adaptation reports an announced event; as of 21 September, its discussions and outcomes are still ahead.

Space-based solar powerCollecting solar energy in space and transmitting it for use elsewhere, including Earth. would collect sunlight beyond Earth and transmit energy wirelessly to receiving equipment below. NASA’s January 2024 assessment describes it as a potential future energy source and identifies major engineering gaps, including assembling and maintaining large systems in orbit and efficient power transmission. A concept drawing can show that chain, but each link must work as part of a usable system.

There are experimental results to discuss. Caltech reported in January 2024 that its completed SSPD-1 mission had tested solar cells, a deployable structure and wireless power transfer. The team demonstrated transmission in space and directed a signal toward Earth. Those achievements concern prototype technologies; the university’s account still treated electricity supplied at commercial rates as a future prospect.

Cost adds another layer. NASA’s study examined conceptual systems operating in 2050 and found them more expensive than terrestrial sustainable alternatives under its assumptions, while noting that technological improvements could change the comparison. That is a dated scenario analysis, not a current electricity quotation. Launching, manufacturing and maintaining infrastructure are part of the energy question, rather than details that disappear once transmission has been demonstrated.

The organiser intends the symposium to connect space developers with utility, grid and energy professionals. Its advocacy for the technology is clear; SPFI does not adopt the promotional cost or reliability figures as established performance. A useful discussion can distinguish what has been tested, what has only been modelled, and which next measurements would help prospective users judge the proposal.

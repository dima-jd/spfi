The grid decides

Three Electricity Authority moves in October and November 2026 decide which solar projects can connect, how much they earn, and who pays when the grid can’t take their power.

Select an underlined term for a short explanation.

The cheapest part of a solar project is often the panels. The hard part is the connection: the right to push electricity into a crowded grid and be paid for it. Between October and November 2026, Israel’s Electricity Authority made three moves that rewrite those rights for large and small projects alike.

Large projects: more room, less certainty. Decision 75104, dated 18 October, opens a second 2,000 MW quota for renewable plants, including storage, connected to the transmission grid. It runs until filled or until 30 June 2028. But a connection study can now limit how much a plant may export or charge. During such a constraint period the plant is not compensated for energy it cannot produce. The decision also cuts the Capacity creditthe share of a plant’s capacity the system counts on during peak demand. for storage beyond the plant’s solar output to 15%. In plain terms, a megawatt of connection no longer guarantees a megawatt of income.

Small and mid-size projects: a falling tariff and a queue that costs money. A consultation published on 19 October proposes extending the low-voltage framework to 2030. The tariff would drop in two steps during 2027 and then about 5% a year. The size ceiling for tariff support would shrink from 630 kW in 2027 to 300 kW in 2030; larger systems would sell through the market, including via private electricity suppliers who receive a premium. From January 2028, scarce grid capacity would be allocated by economic contribution rather than first-come, first-served. Rooftops up to 15 kW keep their fast track. The Authority’s own figures show about 2,323 MW already connected under the current framework.

Built-up areas: a premium with an end date. A separate consultation proposes letting generation and generation-plus-storage projects join the Urban premiuman extra payment for generation built in or near built-up areas., a bonus for projects near demand centres, until the end of 2030. Payments would stop on 31 December 2037, so slower projects would earn the premium for fewer years.

Together these rules reward projects that are fast, flexible and close to consumption. That favours industrial roofs with their own load and storage. It may disadvantage public canopies and municipal buildings, whose procurement is slow and whose structures cost more per kilowatt. SPFI will test the final decisions against those cases.

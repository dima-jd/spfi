Storage moves into planning

Israel has about 600 MW of batteries and plans for roughly 9,000 MW by 2030. Two drafts this autumn decide where they can go and who is allowed to build them.

Solar panels produce most at midday. Israelis use most electricity in the evening. Batteries bridge that gap, and Israel now plans to build a lot of them. A draft national storage policy, published for comment in November 2026, says the country has about 600 MW of lithium-ion batteries next to solar fields, plus two Pumped storagestoring energy by pumping water uphill and releasing it through turbines. plants: Gilboa (300 MW) and Kochav HaYarden (344 MW). The Electricity Authority projects around 9,250 MW of storage, about 40,200 MWh, by 2030. At a November conference the Energy Ministry’s director-general put the target at about 10,000 MW.

Where batteries may stand. On 6 October the National Planning Council sent an amendment to the storage chapter of the national master plan (TAMA 1Israel’s consolidated national master plan for infrastructure.) out for public objections. It would allow storage beside solar on reservoirs and fish ponds, even in protected landscape areas, subject to review, and would ease storage in industrial zones. It is not yet approved. Fire safety is a big cost driver. Lithium-ion batteries can overheat uncontrollably, and the Fire and Rescue Authority requires strict standards and large water reserves even in open farmland.

Who may build them. The ministry’s draft policy proposes replacing project-by-project quotas with national targets by voltage level and region, monitored over time. It also describes two practical routes for businesses and farms. In the Limited-export routea proposed connection type that caps what a site may send to the grid., a system up to 630 kW may export no more than 15 kW to the grid; the rest must be stored or used on site, with a 150% penalty tariff for excess export. In the self-consumption route, storage up to 8 MW is allowed if it never exports, sized at up to 20% of the existing connection.

Those routes matter most where the grid says no. The draft admits that distribution operators often refuse or delay low-voltage storage, sometimes for years, because they lack the software to manage it. A factory roof or a packhouse could use its own panels and battery without waiting for export capacity.

The open questions are spatial. Batteries are industrial objects with fences, cooling and fire roads. Placed beside a reservoir, a sports field or a village edge, they change the place. SPFI will look for where the final rules put them, and who gets to object.

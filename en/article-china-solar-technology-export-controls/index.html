China locks up the recipe, not the panels

China has proposed banning exports of its most advanced solar-manufacturing know-how. Israeli projects can still buy panels. Israeli factories and licensing deals may find the door closing.

Select an underlined term for a short explanation.

Almost every solar panel on an Israeli roof or field was made with Chinese technology. On 16 October 2026, China’s commerce and science ministries proposed adding 12 photovoltaic manufacturing technologies to the country’s catalogue of technologies prohibited or restricted from export. The proposal is part of a wider list of 40 technologies, including AI and biomedicine.

What would be banned. Proposed outright prohibitions cover manufacturing processes for perovskite and Perovskite-silicon tandema cell that stacks a perovskite layer on silicon to capture more of the light spectrum. cells and modules, the next generation of high-efficiency solar. They also cover very large fluidised-bed Polysiliconthe purified silicon from which wafers are cut. plants (lines of at least 250,000 tonnes a year) and wafers 70 micrometres thick or thinner.

What would be restricted. Licences would be required for polysilicon engineering for lines of at least 30,000 tonnes a year, wafers of 130 micrometres or less, and high-efficiency TOPCon, HJT, xBCtoday’s leading high-efficiency silicon cell designs., back-contact (xBC) and heterojunction (HJT) cell manufacturing. Associated key equipment is included. Exporters must already pre-register covered transfers: that interim requirement took effect immediately. The public consultation closed on 15 November; SPFI found no final catalogue by 20 November.

What it means for Israel. The controls target know-how and production equipment, not finished panels. Israeli developers buying modules for farms, roofs and canopies should see little direct effect, at least for now. The impact falls on a different group: anyone trying to manufacture cells or specialised modules outside China, or to license Chinese process technology. That includes the lightweight, flexible and building-integrated products that Israeli companies are developing for canopies and weak roofs (see “Shade as public infrastructure”), and any plan to localise part of the supply chain.

Two practical questions follow. Should long-lived public projects specify performance and replaceable parts rather than one supplier’s cell technology? And should Israeli research and manufacturing policy plan for a world in which the most advanced production recipes are no longer for sale?

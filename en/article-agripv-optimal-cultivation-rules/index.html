AgriPV gets a referee

Israel has drafted the rules that decide whether farming under solar panels is real. The penalties are financial, and the last step is dismantling.

Select an underlined term for a short explanation.

For years, an Israeli AgriPV project had to promise that farming would continue under its panels. In September 2026, two regulators drafted what happens if it doesn’t. The Ministry of Agriculture published a draft procedure defining “Optimal agricultural cultivationthe Ministry of Agriculture’s test for whether farming under an AgriPV system meets minimum standards.”. The Electricity Authority opened a complementary hearing that ties that definition to the tariff a project is paid.

The draft sets measurable floors. A law-firm analysis published in Calcalist summarises them: yield or revenue of at least 75% of the regional norm for the same crop, the irrigation the crop needs, and at least 85% of the cultivated area kept in production. Projects that meet the floors are judged mainly on numbers. Projects that miss them move to a second track, where a ministry agronomist reviews how the crop is actually managed: irrigation, fertilisation, pruning, harvest.

Reporting becomes a yearly duty. Developers would file agricultural and financial data, drawn from field records, water meters and sensors. Late or inaccurate reports can count as non-compliance in themselves. Projects above 300 dunams would also host a 15-dunam research plot, led by a principal investigator with a doctorate.

The consequences escalate over time. Under the proposal, a first year of failure brings a warning. Two consecutive years bring a Tariff reductiona cut in the price paid per kilowatt-hour, used here as a penalty. of 5–10%, and a third year a further 15–20%. After four years, the ministry may recommend that planning authorities dismantle the facility. Developers keep a right to a hearing and a reasoned objection.

The percentages are proposals, not law. The comment period has closed, and SPFI found no final procedure or tariff decision published by 20 November 2026. Reported deadlines also differ between sources, which is another reason to wait for the final text before treating any figure as binding.

The real story is a shift in who carries agricultural risk. A developer’s revenue would now depend on what grows under the array, not only on what the array generates. That makes the contract between landowner, farmer and developer the place to watch: who pays when a crop fails, and who decides what is planted?

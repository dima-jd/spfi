Solar shade needs more than one season of testing

A Massachusetts experiment challenges promises of a universal crop benefit.

Select an underlined term for a short explanation.

At a Massachusetts research farm, vegetables grew beneath solar panels and in full sun. Kate Doubleday and colleagues examined the 2016–2018 seasons, asking how crops responded when panels intercepted part of their light.

This shared use is called AgrivoltaicsAgriculture and solar electricity production sharing the same land.. The US Department of Energy explains that design choices shape what farming remains possible. Taller structures can improve access but require more supporting material. More space between panels or rows can admit light and equipment, while reducing the electricity available from a given area. A design that looks efficient on an energy plan therefore needs to be examined from the farmer’s side too.

The paper, published in 2025, found higher Marketable yieldThe harvested portion suitable for sale, rather than all plant material. in full sun for all four crops in the warm, wet 2018 season. Broccoli observations were missing for 2016. Wider panel gaps did not consistently help every crop. The results make a single-season success story an unreliable guide to a long-lived installation.

The broader opportunity remains worth investigating. DOE describes potential irrigation benefits and new farm income, alongside higher construction costs and unresolved business questions. Keeping agricultural land productive involves more than placing crops in a photograph: farmers, developers and communities need workable arrangements and shared goals. These are potential benefits and design challenges, not evidence that every agrivoltaic project will earn more money.

The most useful question is therefore more specific than whether solar shade is good or bad. Which crop, under which design, and in which season? A Control plotA comparison area without the treatment being tested; here, without panel shading. helps reveal what changed, while repeated harvests show whether the result lasts. SPFI’s reading is that crop records and electricity figures should be examined together, with enough detail to distinguish a promising combination from a result that depended on one favourable year.

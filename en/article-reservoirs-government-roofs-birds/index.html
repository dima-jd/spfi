Reservoirs, government roofs and the birds

Water reservoirs look like the perfect solar surface: flat, unused, owned by the state. A new Israeli wildlife study says they are not empty.

Israel has hundreds of water reservoirs, many of them operational rather than scenic. To an energy planner they look ideal: flat surfaces that consume no farmland, next to pumps that use a lot of electricity. In July 2026 Mekorot, the national water company, invited developers to DBOdesign-build-operate contract. up to ten solar installations with batteries on its reservoirs. The Jerusalem Post reports a combined capacity of about 100 MW with four to six hours of storage. Mekorot would have first right to buy the power for its own pumping. It says more than 250 MW of its projects are in development. The tender has closed; the radar found no award yet.

The wildlife data. The Israel Nature and Parks Authority has published a study linking solar arrays on reservoirs with a decline of up to 40% in observed duck and diving-bird species. Filter-feeding ducks and divers were hit hardest. Rare species are among them: Israel hosts about a third of the world’s wintering white-headed ducks, according to the report. The authority recommends that key reservoirs stay off-limits to panels, that the covered share of each water surface be capped, and that monitoring continue.

That changes the reservoir story from “free surface” to “shared habitat”. Not every reservoir matters equally to birds, and not every installation covers the same share of water. But the evidence argues for choosing sites before writing tenders, not after.

Government roofs. In parallel, the Finance Ministry’s Government Housing Administration ran a tender in 2026 for privately financed rooftop PV on government buildings, explicitly including BIPVbuilding-integrated photovoltaics: panels that replace roof or façade materials rather than sitting on top of them. (BIPV). Submissions closed in September. No award, site list or design standard has been published. BIPV would make panels part of façades and roofs, which raises questions about waterproofing, maintenance access and heritage buildings.

The common thread is that public assets come with public functions. A reservoir stores water and hosts birds. A government building carries architecture and civic identity. SPFI will look at whether the winning designs protect those functions and publish what they cover.

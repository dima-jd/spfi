When “dual use” loses the harvest

A Chinese wheat field lost over 40% of its yield under a solar array. A Californian lettuce field did better in summer. The difference is the lesson for Israel.

Select an underlined term for a short explanation.

AgriPV is often sold as a win-win: the same field produces food and power. Two 2026 field studies show how much depends on the crop, the season and the shade.

Wheat in Jiangsu, China. Researchers studied winter wheat under a “flexible” AgriPV system: modules strung on cables over 30-metre spans, 4 metres above the ground, so combine harvesters can pass beneath. The design worked for machines. It did not work for wheat. Panels covered 65% of the area, and monthly solar radiation reaching the crop fell by 57–62%. Over two seasons, yields were 42–47% lower than in open field, both under and between the panels. The authors name light as the main limit and recommend Coverage ratiothe share of ground area shaded by panels when seen from above., wider spacing and Shade-tolerant cropa crop whose yield falls little when light is reduced..

Lettuce in Pomona, California. At Cal Poly Pomona’s Spadra Farm, students have grown and weighed 3,556 heads of romaine lettuce under and beside panels across five crop cycles since summer 2025. In spring, leaves under the panels lost about half as much water. Yields under panels were lower in the cooler seasons of 2025, but by summer 2026 shaded lettuce weighed as much as or more than open-field lettuce. The university calls these early results from one farm, without published statistics.

What the two have in common. Both are honest about trade-offs. Shade costs yield when light is the limiting factor: winter wheat, cool-season lettuce. Shade can pay back when heat and water are the limiting factors: summer lettuce in a hot, dry climate. Israel’s own tomato trials (see “Israel’s AgriPV evidence gap”) found the same pattern across rows: heavy shade cut yield by 40–55%, light shade barely at all.

For Israeli regulation, that suggests three things. Yield norms should be crop- and season-specific, not a single national percentage. Panel coverage and height are agricultural decisions, not only engineering ones. And the label “dual use” should come after measured results, not before.
